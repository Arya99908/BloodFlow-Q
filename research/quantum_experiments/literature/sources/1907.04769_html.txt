 Abstract

 Hybrid quantum/classical variational algorithms can be implemented on noisy intermediate-scale quantum computers and can be used to find solutions for combinatorial optimization problems.
Approaches discussed in the literature minimize the expectation of the problem Hamiltonian for a parameterized trial quantum state.
The expectation is estimated as the sample mean of a set of measurement outcomes, while the parameters of the trial state are optimized classically.
This procedure is fully justified for quantum mechanical observables such as molecular energies.
In the case of classical optimization problems, which yield diagonal Hamiltonians, we argue that aggregating the samples in a different way than the expected value is more natural.
In this paper we propose the Conditional Value-at-Risk as an aggregation function.
We empirically show – using classical simulation as well as quantum hardware – that this leads to faster convergence to better solutions for all combinatorial optimization problems tested in our study.
We also provide analytical results to explain the observed difference in performance between different variational algorithms.

 1 Introduction

 Combinatorial optimization (CO) has been extensively studied, because
it is applied in many areas of business and science, and it is
a source of interesting mathematical problems
 [ nemhauser1988 ] . Even if many CO problems are NP-hard, they are
routinely solved (possibly not to provable optimality) in industrial
applications. It is widely believed that quantum computers cannot
solve such problems in polynomial time (i.e., NP ⊈ \not\subseteq BQP
 [ bernstein1997quantum ] ), but there is significant effort toward
designing quantum algorithms that could be practically useful by
quickly finding near-optimal solutions to these hard problems. Among
these algorithms, two candidates are more likely to be efficiently
implementable on noisy quantum computers: the
Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)
 [ moll2018quantum , fried2017qtorch ] , and the Quantum Approximate
Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
 [ Farhi2014 , farhi2017quantum , crooks2018performance ] . Both VQE and
QAOA use a parametrized quantum circuit U ⁡ ( θ ) U(\theta) (also called
variational form) to generate trial wave functions | ψ ⁡ ( θ ) ⟩ = U ⁡ ( θ ) ​ | 0 ⟩ \ket{\psi(\theta)}=U(\theta)\ket{0} , guided by a classical optimization algorithm that
aims to solve:

 min θ ⁡ ⟨ ψ ⁡ ( θ ) | ​ H ​ | ψ ⁡ ( θ ) ⟩ . \displaystyle\min_{\theta}\bra{\psi(\theta)}H\ket{\psi(\theta)}. 

 (1) 

 This expression encodes the total energy of a system through its
Hamiltonian H H , and more specifically its expected value
 ⟨ ψ ⁡ ( θ ) | ​ H ​ | ψ ⁡ ( θ ) ⟩ \bra{\psi(\theta)}H\ket{\psi(\theta)} . There is a well-known
transformation to map CO problems into a Hamiltonian, see e.g.,
 [ lucas2014ising ] ; this will also be discussed subsequently in
this paper. Empirical evaluations indicate that VQE’s performance
suffers from some key weaknesses in the context of CO problems, and
there is significant room for improvement [ Nannicini2019 ] . At the
same time, the literature on QAOA paints a mixed picture: while some
work concludes that QAOA has promise
 [ crooks2018performance , niu2019optimizing ] , other papers indicate
that it may not perform better than classical algorithms
 [ hastings2019classical ] .

 In this paper, we propose a modification to the problem
( 1 ) that is given to the classical
optimization algorithm: rather than minimizing the expected value
 ⟨ ψ ⁡ ( θ ) | ​ H ​ | ψ ⁡ ( θ ) ⟩ \bra{\psi(\theta)}H\ket{\psi(\theta)} , we minimize its Conditional
Value-at-Risk (CVaR) – a measure that takes into account only the
tail of the probability distribution and is widely used in finance
 [ acerbi2002coherence ] . We argue that CVaR more closely matches
the practical goal of heuristic solution methods for CO problems. We
provide an analytical and numerical study, showing that the proposed
methodology significantly improves performance and robustness of VQE
and QAOA in the context of CO. As a byproduct of this analysis, we
find that for small-depth circuits it is important to use variational
forms with a sufficient number of parameters, e.g., at least linear in
the number of qubits. QAOA for fixed (small) depth does not satisfy
this requirement, and produces “flat” quantum states that yield low
probability of sampling the optimal solution. Thus, not only does our
paper introduce a simple way to improve the performance of the two
most prominent candidates for CO on noisy quantum computers, but it
also sheds some light on their behavior with small-depth circuits.
While we do not claim that this brings their performance on par with
state-of-the-art classical heuristics, our results indicate a marked
improvement as compared to what is proposed in the literature. The
numerical experiments discussed in this paper are implemented in the
open-source library Qiskit [ Qiskit ] , and executed on classical
quantum simulators as well as IBM’s quantum hardware.

 The rest of this paper is divided as follows. The next two sections
set the context for this paper by giving an overview of key concepts
from the literature: the mapping of CO problems as a Hamiltonian, VQE
(Section 2 ), and QAOA (Section 3 ). Section
 4 formally introduces our main contribution, CVaR
optimization. An analysis of the method is given in Section
 5 . Section provides
an empirical evaluation of the proposed method on classically
simulated quantum circuits, and using quantum hardware. Section
 provides a formal analysis of certain aspects
of QAOA to explain the experimentally observed behavior. Finally,
Section concludes the paper.

 2 Variational Quantum Eigensolver

 The Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) is a hybrid
quantum/classical algorithm originally proposed to approximate the
ground state of a quantum chemical system, i.e., the state attaining
the minimum energy [ Peruzzo2014 ] . This is achieved by solving a
problem of the form ( 1 ), with H H encoding
the total energy of the quantum chemical system. The same approach can
be used to attempt to solve CO problems. Consider a quadratic
unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problem on n n variables:

 min x ∈ { 0 , 1 } n ⁡ b T ​ x + x T ​ A ​ x , \displaystyle\min_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}b^{T}x+x^{T}Ax, 

 (2) 

 for given b ∈ ℝ n b\in\mathbb{R}^{n} and A ∈ ℝ n × n A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} .
Using the variable transformation x i = ( 1 − z i ) / 2 x_{i}=(1-z_{i})/2 for z i ∈ { − 1 , + 1 } z_{i}\in\{-1,+1\} , problem ( 2 ) can be expressed as an Ising spin
glass model:

 min z ∈ { − 1 , + 1 } n ⁡ c T ​ z + z T ​ Q ​ z , \displaystyle\min_{z\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}}c^{T}z+z^{T}Qz, 

 (3) 

 where c c and Q Q are easily computed from ( 2 ). These two
equivalent problems encompass binary optimization problems, i.e., CO
problems, and they are NP-hard [ barahona1982computational ] .

 Problem ( 3 ) can be translated into a Hamiltonian for an
 n n -qubit system: we replace z i z_{i} by the Pauli Z-matrix

 σ Z i \displaystyle\sigma_{Z}^{i} 
 = \displaystyle= 
 ( 1 0 0 − 1 ) \displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right) 

 acting on the i i -th qubit, and each term of the form z i ​ z j z_{i}z_{j} by
 σ Z i ⊗ σ Z j \sigma_{Z}^{i}\otimes\sigma_{Z}^{j} . The summation of (tensor products of) Pauli terms obtained this way is the desired Hamiltonian. The eigenvalues ± 1 \pm 1 of
 σ Z i \sigma_{Z}^{i} correspond to the positive and negative spin of
( 3 ). After constructing H H , we can apply VQE to attempt
to determine the ground state, from which an optimal solution to
( 3 ) can be sampled with probability 1.

 The choice of the variational form U ⁡ ( θ ) U(\theta) is important. In this
paper we follow a standard approach, see e.g.,
 [ kandala2017hardware , Nannicini2019 ] . Assuming n n qubits, we
start by applying single-qubit Y-rotations to every qubit,
parametrized by an angle θ 0 , i \theta_{0,i} for qubit i i . We then repeat
the following p p times: we apply controlled Z-gates to all qubit
pairs ( i , j ) (i,j) satisfying i < j i<j , where i i denotes the control qubit
and j j the target qubit; and we add another layer of single-qubit Y-rotations to every qubit, parametrized by θ k , i \theta_{k,i} for qubit i i 
and repetition k ∈ { 1 , … , p } k\in\{1,\dots,p\} .
Fig.  1 illustrates such a variational form for n = 3 n=3 and p = 2 p=2 .
Notice that this variational form span all basis states. Overall, this leads to
 n ⁡ ( 1 + p ) n(1+p) parametrized Y-rotations, and n ⁡ ( n − 1 ) 2 ​ p \frac{n(n-1)}{2}p controlled
Z-gates. Since the controlled Z-gates commute with each other, the
total circuit depth is O ⁡ ( n ​ p ) O(np) , although the number of gates is
quadratic in n n . This variational form is used in our numerical simulations. When experimenting on quantum hardware
we use nearest neighbor controlled Z-gates rather than all-to-all, i.e., we follow the connectivity provided by the hardware to
reduce the number of two-qubit gates. We remark that these variational forms span any basis state (and thus the ground state of a diagonal Hamiltonian) already with the first layer of Y-rotations. The purpose of subsequent layers is to hopefully facilitate the task for the classical optimization algorithm used to choose the variational parameters. The above variational form with just one layer of Y-rotations can be efficiently simulated classically; for a discussion on the effect of entangling gates, we refer to [ Nannicini2019 ] .

 The classical optimization algorithm that attempts to solve
( 1 ) must be able to (at least) compute the
objective function, i.e., the expected value in
( 1 ). This is difficult to compute directly,
but it can be estimated as follows. First, we prepare the trial
wavefunction | ψ ⁡ ( θ ) ⟩ \ket{\psi(\theta)} on a quantum processor. Then, we
measure the qubits, resulting in an n n -bit string x 0 ​ … ​ x n − 1 x_{0}\dots x_{n-1} . Each observed string easily translates into a sample from
 ⟨ ψ ⁡ ( θ ) | ​ H ​ | ψ ⁡ ( θ ) ⟩ \bra{\psi(\theta)}H\ket{\psi(\theta)} , because H H is a weighted
summation of tensor products of Pauli Z-matrices, and each such term
can be computed with a simple parity check.
We denote these samples by H k ​ ( θ ) H_{k}(\theta) , k = 1 , … , K k=1,\dots,K , where K ∈ ℕ K\in\mathbb{N} is the number
of samples. The sample mean

 1 K ​ ∑ k = 1 K H k ​ ( θ ) \displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}H_{k}(\theta) 

 (7) 

 is an estimator for ⟨ ψ ⁡ ( θ ) | ​ H ​ | ψ ⁡ ( θ ) ⟩ \bra{\psi(\theta)}H\ket{\psi(\theta)} , and is
used as the objective function for the classical optimization
algorithm. The solution returned by the algorithm is then given by the
bitstring that leads to the smallest H k H_{k} among all observed
bitstrings and all θ \theta that have been evaluated.

 3 Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm

 The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is a hybrid
quantum/classical algorithm specifically developed for CO problems. In
the context of this paper, QAOA can be seen as a form of VQE with a
specific choice of the variational form that is derived from the
problem Hamiltonian H H [ Farhi2014 ] . QAOA enjoys stronger
convergence properties than VQE. For some problems, it can be shown
that QAOA determines a quantum state with a guaranteed approximation
ratio with respect to the ground state
 [ Farhi2014 , farhi2014e3lin2 ] ; furthermore, QAOA applies adiabatic
evolution as the circuit depth goes to ∞ \infty , implying that it will
determine the optimal solution of the CO problem if the depth of the
variational form is large enough and we can find the optimal circuit
parameters.

 Given a problem Hamiltonian corresponding to a QUBO as defined in
( 3 ), the variational form of QAOA is constructed with a
layer of Hadamard gates, followed by two alternating unitaries:

 = \displaystyle= 
 e − i ​ γ ​ ( ∑ i = 1 n c i ​ σ Z i + ∑ i , j = 1 n Q i ​ j ​ σ Z i ⊗ σ Z j ) , \displaystyle e^{-i\gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\sigma_{Z}^{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}Q_{ij}\sigma_{Z}^{i}\otimes\sigma_{Z}^{j}\right)}, 

 (8) 

 U B ​ ( β ) \displaystyle U_{B}(\beta) 
 = \displaystyle= 
 e − i β ∑ i = 1 n σ X i , \displaystyle e^{-i\beta\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{X}^{i}}, 

 (9) 

 where σ X i \sigma_{X}^{i} denotes the Pauli X-matrix

 σ X i \displaystyle\sigma_{X}^{i} 
 = \displaystyle= 
 ( 0 1 1 0 ) \displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right) 

 acting on the i i -th qubit.

is thus defined as:

 (13) 

 where β , γ ∈ ℝ p \beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{p} are vectors of variational
parameters, and here, H H denotes a Hadamard gate. (The symbol H H is overloaded in the quantum computing literature; in this paper it generally denotes the Hamiltonian, and in the few occasions where it indicates a Hadamard gate, we note it explicitly.)
This yields the trial wave function:

 | ψ ⁡ ( β , γ ) ⟩ \displaystyle\ket{\psi(\beta,\gamma)} 
 = \displaystyle=

 which replaces | ψ ⁡ ( θ ) ⟩ \ket{\psi(\theta)} in ( 1 ).
As in VQE, the expected value is computed as the sample mean over
multiple observations from the quantum state, and the algorithm
returns the bitstring that leads to the smallest H k H_{k} among all
observed bitstrings and choices of β , γ \beta,\gamma .

Hamiltonian, which is a difference with respect to VQE.

be better than the worst case if Q Q is sparse (i.e., there are few
two-qubit interactions).

 4 CVaR Optimization

 In quantum mechanics observables are defined as expected values
 ⟨ ψ | ​ H ​ | ψ ⟩ \bra{\psi}H\ket{\psi} . This leads to the natural choice of the
sample mean ( 7 ) as the objective function for the
classical optimization problems embedded in VQE and QAOA. We argue
that for problems with a diagonal Hamiltonian, such as CO problems,
the sample mean may be a poor choice in practice. This is because when
 H H is diagonal, there exists a ground state which is a basis
state. If determining the value H j , j H_{j,j} of a basis state | j ⟩ \ket{j} 
is classically easy, the state with the minimum eigenvalue computed by
an algorithm is simply the best measurement outcome among all
measurements performed. It is therefore reasonable to focus on
improving the properties of the best measurement outcome, rather than
the average. We illustrate this intuition with a simple
example. Consider two algorithms 𝒜 1 {\cal A}_{1} and 𝒜 2 {\cal A}_{2} applied
to a problem with Hamiltonian H H , minimum eigenvalue λ min \lambda_{\min} 
and ground state | j ⟩ \ket{j} . Suppose 𝒜 1 {\cal A}_{1} produces a state
 | ψ 1 ⟩ \ket{\psi_{1}} and 𝒜 2 {\cal A}_{2} produces a state | ψ 2 ⟩ \ket{\psi_{2}} , with
the following properties: ⟨ ψ 1 | ​ H ​ | ψ 1 ⟩ / λ min = 1.1 \bra{\psi_{1}}H\ket{\psi_{1}}/\lambda_{\min}=1.1 and ⟨ j | ψ 1 ⟩ = 0 \braket{j|\psi_{1}}=0 ; ⟨ ψ 2 | ​ H ​ | ψ 2 ⟩ / λ min = 2.0 \bra{\psi_{2}}H\ket{\psi_{2}}/\lambda_{\min}=2.0 and ⟨ j | ψ 2 ⟩ = 0.1 \braket{j|\psi_{2}}=0.1 . We argue that
from a practical point of view, algorithm 𝒜 2 {\cal A}_{2} is likely to be
more useful than 𝒜 1 {\cal A}_{1} . This is because even if 𝒜 1 {\cal A}_{1} 
leads to samples with a better objective function value on average,
 𝒜 1 {\cal A}_{1} will never yield the ground state | j ⟩ \ket{j} ; whereas
 𝒜 2 {\cal A}_{2} , which is much worse on average, has a positive and
sufficiently high probability of yielding the ground state, so that
with enough repetitions we can be almost certain of determining
 | j ⟩ \ket{j} .

 In light of this discussion, one way to attain our goal would be to
choose, as the objective function, the minimum observed outcome over a set
of measurements: min ⁡ { H 1 , … , H K } \min\{H_{1},...,H_{K}\} .
However, for finite K K this typically leads to a non-smooth,
ill-behaved objective function that is difficult to handle for
classical optimization algorithms.

 To help smooth the objective function, while still focusing on
improving the best measured outcomes rather than the average, we
propose the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR, also called Expected
Shortfall) as the objective function. Formally, the CVaR of a random variable
 X X for a confidence level α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \alpha\in(0,1] is defined as

 CVaR α ​ ( X ) \displaystyle\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X) 
 = \displaystyle= 
 𝔼 ⁡ [ X | X ≤ F X − 1 ​ ( α ) ] \displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X|X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(\alpha)] 

 (15) 

 where F X F_{X} denotes the cumulative density function of X X . In other
words, CVaR is the expected value of the lower α \alpha -tail of the
distribution of X X . Without loss of generality, assume that the
samples H k H_{k} are sorted in nondecreasing order, i.e. H k + 1 ≥ H k H_{k+1}\geq H_{k} . Then, for a given set of samples { H k } k = 1 , … , K \{H_{k}\}_{k=1,\dots,K} and
value of α \alpha , the CVaR α \text{CVaR}_{\alpha} is defined as

 1 ⌈ α ​ K ⌉ ​ ∑ k = 0 ⌈ α ​ K ⌉ H k . \displaystyle\frac{1}{\lceil\alpha K\rceil}\sum_{k=0}^{\lceil\alpha K\rceil}H_{k}. 

 (16) 

 Note that the limit α ↘ 0 \alpha\searrow 0 corresponds to the minimum, and α = 1 \alpha=1 
corresponds to the expected value of X X . In this sense, CVaR is a
generalization of both the sample mean ( 7 ),
and the best observed sample min ⁡ { H 1 , … , H K } \min\{H_{1},...,H_{K}\} . For small,
nonzero values of α \alpha , CVaR still puts emphasis on the best
observed samples, but it leads to a smoother and easier to handle
objective function. It is clear that this can be applied to both VQE
and QAOA, simply by replacing the sample mean
( 7 ) with CVaR α \text{CVaR}_{\alpha} in the
classical optimization algorithm. We call the resulting algorithms
 CVaR -VQE and CVaR -QAOA,
respectively.

 5 Analysis of CVaR Optimization

 The optimization of CVaR α \text{CVaR}_{\alpha} with α < 1 \alpha<1 modifies the
